Wander

A Gen Z take on TripAdvisor: a travel life-logging app where people log places they've been, plan trips, and discover destinations through other travelers' real experiences.

The problem

No place to plan from real experiences.

Travel tools today either handle logistics or show curated highlights, and neither helps you plan from real, personal experiences.

Booking apps

Expedia and Booking.com organize itineraries but leave out reflection and personality.

Social media

Instagram and TikTok capture trips, but they're highly curated and hard to turn into genuine recommendations.

There was no single place to reflect on where you've been and use that to plan where to go next. Our biggest inspiration was Beli, which has become a Gen Z alternative to Yelp: you rank restaurants you've been to and get recommendations based on your taste and your friends' activity. Travel had no equivalent. TripAdvisor works, but it feels built for a different generation.

We set out to make the Beli of travel: social, personal, and built around people you trust.

Design goals

Four things it had to do.

1Make logging easyRecording a place, with photos, a rating, a description, and tags like "nature" or "free to enter," should feel quick, not like homework.
2Make planning collaborativeTrip lists should be easy to build and share with the people you're traveling with.
3Make discovery feel familiarBrowsing other people's logs should feel like the social apps people already use.
4Make it accessibleReadable contrast and comfortable sizing throughout.
Key design decisions

Four calls that shaped the app.

Decision 01 · Navigation

A side menu modeled on patterns people already know

The hardest structural question was what belonged in the pull-out side menu: which pages, which actions, and how much account information. I looked to Instagram, since our users already know its patterns, and split navigation into two layers. The bottom tab bar holds the five things people do most: Home, Trips, Post, Map, and Profile. The side menu holds secondary and account items: Settings, Places been, Saved, Log out, and a contact line. That kept the main screen focused on content.

Decision 02 · Logging

Tags to make logs useful later

Each log captures photos, a rating, a description, and tags like "nature," "group-friendly," or "free to enter." Tags turn personal memories into something other travelers can filter and plan from.

Decision 03 · Tradeoff

Shipping the map with React Leaflet over Google Maps

I wanted the map to feel modern and familiar, ideally with Google Maps. With the time we had, I shipped it with React Leaflet instead. It works, but it looks dated. It was the right call for the deadline, and it's the first thing I'd upgrade.

The shipped version uses React Leaflet

Decision 04 · Accessibility

Accessibility as a requirement, not a polish step

Feedback from our professor, classmates, and friends consistently pointed to color contrast and sizing. I adjusted contrast and increased the size of text and key elements so the app stays readable and usable for more people.

For example, in the prototype the side-menu items used mauve on the cream background, about 2.7:1 contrast, well under the 4.5:1 WCAG target for body text. In the build, most text uses the palette's navy, at about 12.9:1.

Process

Figma to HTML to React.

I went from a mobile Figma prototype to a static HTML draft to the final React app.

  1. Figma prototype: designed 17 mobile screens, from splash and sign-in through the feed, comments, logging, trips (including inviting collaborators by email), map, profile, saved places, and settings. I built the visual system from a 5-color palette, navy and slate for text and structure, dusty rose and cream for surfaces. In a later pass I added a Recommendations filter to the feed and map, tying back to the Beli-style idea of planning from people you trust.
  2. Static HTML draft: built early versions of the key pages to test layout and flow before adding real data.
  3. React build: used Figma Dev Mode for exact specs and Claude Code to translate designs into components quickly, then wired up Firebase for sign-in and data.

Using Dev Mode and Claude Code together let me spend less time hand-translating spacing and styles, and more time on the flows themselves.

Feedback & iteration

Easier to read, easier to use.

We shared the concept and designs with our professor, classmates, and friends, and the most consistent feedback was about accessibility.

Reflection & next steps

What didn't make the cut.

We scoped down to ship on time, and a few features didn't make it.

Google Maps integrationReplace React Leaflet for a more modern, familiar map.
Your own posts on your profileRight now you can post, but you can't view your posts from your profile.
Your own trips viewA dedicated view for trips you've created.
Test with real travelersOur feedback came from people around us. Next I'd test logging and trip planning with people actively planning a trip.

Borrowing patterns people already know made the app easier to pick up, and freed me to spend design time on what was actually new.
