Case study · iOS · March 2026

Sublease-go

An iOS app that makes subletting easier for UW students, with verified accounts, filterable listings, and messaging built into each listing.

Role
Product designer & frontend lead. Designed the low-fi and high-fi prototypes and built all UI screens in SwiftUI.
Team
4 students
Timeline
2 weeks, March 2026
iOS mobile development course
Tools
Figma, SwiftUI, Firebase, Claude, Claude Code
The problem

Subletting runs on tools never built for it.

UW students find sublets through Facebook groups, Reddit, and word of mouth, and none of those were built for it.

No verification

Anyone can post a listing, so you can't tell if you're talking to a real student.

Hard to keep track of

Listings are scattered across platforms and buried in feeds and comment threads.

No easy way to compare

Key details like dates, price, and whether it's furnished live in free-text posts, if they're there at all.

Design goals

One goal per pain point.

1TrustEvery user should be a verified UW student.
2FitStudents should be able to tell if a place works for them before reaching out.
3ContactGetting in touch with a lister should take as few steps as possible.
Key design decisions

Four calls that shaped the app.

Decision 01 · Trust

Verify with a UW email, and keep it simple

We wanted Sublease-go to be for UW students only, so sign-in requires a @uw.edu email, and profiles show a "UW Verified" badge. We'd talked about more extensive verification, but with 2 weeks, email-based auth gave us the trust we needed without eating the timeline. New users get an account automatically on first sign-in, so there's no separate sign-up flow.

Decision 02 · Fit

Put the details students decide on up front

Every listing captures price, bedrooms, building, furnished or not, lease dates, and roommate preferences (gender and school year). The feed can be filtered by max price, minimum bedrooms, and furnished-only, so students can rule places out before messaging anyone.

Decision 03 · Contact

Message from the listing, not the profile

We tested two entry points for contacting a lister: from their profile, or directly on the listing. Putting "Message Lister" on the listing cut a step, since students are already looking at the place they care about. Ongoing conversations live in their own Messages tab.

Decision 04 · Tradeoff

A refresh button over pull-to-refresh

I originally wanted pull-to-refresh on the feed, but couldn't get it working reliably in our timeline. We went with an explicit refresh button instead. It's less elegant, but it's clear and it shipped.

Process

From wireframes to SwiftUI.

I went from low-fi wireframes to high-fi prototypes in Figma, then built every screen in SwiftUI.

  1. Low-fi wireframes to map the core flows: posting a listing, browsing and filtering, managing your listings, and messaging.
  2. High-fi Figma prototypes with the final layout, UW-inspired colors, and a 4-tab structure: Feed, Post, Messages, Profile.
  3. SwiftUI build from the prototypes, with Firebase handling auth, listings, and messages. I used Claude to talk through ideas and Claude Code to prototype screens quickly, then refined the details by hand.

The biggest lesson came in the move from design to build: native iOS components don't always look the way you drew them. The bottom tab bar is a good example. In my low-fi it was a static bar, but iOS renders it as a dynamic, animated control. Designing with platform components in mind from the start would have saved some rework.

Testing & iteration

Three tasks, three changes.

I ran task-based tests with 3 teammates: I gave them a specific task, let them work through the app, and collected feedback afterward. The focus was whether the flow between screens made sense.

What we testedWhat we foundWhat we changed
Contacting a listerGoing through the lister's profile added an extra stepMoved "Message Lister" onto the listing itself
Posting a listingThe new listing appeared in the feed, but the posting screen stayed open, which didn't make senseClosed the posting screen after a successful post
Refreshing the feedPull-to-refresh wasn't working reliablyAdded an explicit refresh button

Testing also surfaced a few bugs that we fixed before the final demo.

Reflection & next steps

If I kept going.

I'd start by testing with UW students outside our team, since teammates already knew how the app was supposed to work.

Test with real usersRecruit students who are actively looking for or posting sublets.
Filter by lease datesDates are captured on every listing but can't be filtered yet, and timing is often the first thing students check.
Bring back pull-to-refreshThe more natural interaction on iOS, now that the core flows are stable.
Stronger verificationGo beyond email domain if the app opened to more users.

Designing for a real platform means designing with its components and constraints, not around them.
